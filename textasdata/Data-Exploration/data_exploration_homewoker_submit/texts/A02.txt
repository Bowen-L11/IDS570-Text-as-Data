Using a game theoretic framework, I model how alliances affect the interactions between nations. I examine a simple three nations system. Suppose that one of these nations, which I call nation A, is contemplating an attack on another nation, nation B. If nation A attacks, then nation B must decide whether to resist. If nation B decides to retaliate then A and B are at war. Once a war starts, the third nation, C, has the option of remaining neutral 316 A. SMITH or intervening in support of B. This paper examines how alliances affect the probability that A attacks ( a ), the probability that B resists (/3), and the probability that C intervenes (y). The model I offer answers several questions: When do nations form alliances? Are alliances reliable? How does an alliance affect the behavior of nations A and B, and hence how are alliances related to the occurrence of war? I concentrate on defensive alliances between nations B and C. By offering nation B an alliance, nation C can potentially deter A from attacking. This is a situation of extended deterrence (Huth, 1988; Wu, 1990). The extended deterrence literature would describe nation A as a "potential attacker", nation B as a "protege" and nation C as a "defender". As well as examining alliance formation, this paper provides a model of deterrence. Alliances are non-binding agreements between nations. Nations pledge to offer military assistance should an ally become involved in a war. In the anarchic system of international relations, alliances can never be binding. However, there are costs associated with the formation and the failure of alliances. These costs could be generated by reputation (Synder and Diesing, 1977; Alt, Calvert and Humes, 1988), by domestic politics (Morrow, 1991; Bueno de Mesquita et al., 1992), or by loss of autonomy (Altfeld, 1988). For the purposes of this paper, the manner in which these costs are produced is irrelevant. What is important is that nations pay costs for forming alliances, and nations pay costs for not honoring their commitments. The model considers formal alliances. However, the results of the model hold for any commitment that is costly to make or costly to break. Any form of costly pledge affects behavior in accordance with the predictions of the model. However, the magnitude of the effects vary with the cost of the commitment. Thus, as well as examining alliance formation, this paper models extended deterrence more generally. The rational choice approach to explaining deterrence has been much debated by both its proponents and its critics (Achen and Snidal, 1990; Downs, 1990; George and Smoke, 1990; Huth and Russett, 1990; Jervis, 1990; Lebow and Stein, 1990; Zagare, 1990). I choose rational choice over other approaches as it enables generalizable statements necessary for theory building to be constructed (Downs, 1990). A theory of alliance behavior needs to explain why some nations form alliances and why others don't. It should also explain why some alliances are reliable and others are not. These seem fairly straightforward questions, but despite the large range of empirical research, the inductive approach has little conclusive to say about how alliances are associated with war. Singer and Small (1967, 1968) were among the first to empirically study EXTENDED DETERRENCE AND ALLIANCES 317 the correlation between the formation of alliances and the occurrence of war. Despite numerous subsequent studies (Ostrom and Hoole, 1978; Levy, 1984; Siverson and Sullivan, 1984; Wayman, 1990; King, 1989; Kegley and Raymond, 1982) there are few robust finding to indicate whether alliance formation makes war more or less likely. The link between alliance formation and the occurrence of war is ambiguous. However, alliances expand conflict. If a nation has an alliance with a combatant, then it is more likely to intervene in a conflict (Sabrosky, 1980; Siverson and King, 1980; Altfeld and Bueno de Mesquita, 1979; Kim, 1991; Oren, 1989; Siverson and Tennefoss, 1984; Smith, 1995a, b). Although alliances significantly increase the probability of intervention, many alliances are unreliable. Empirically, only about 25% of nations intervene on behalf of allies that become involved in wars (Siverson and King, 1980). The low reliability of alliances is worrying in the light of theories of alliance formation. Many of these theories assume that alliances are reliable (Niou and Ordeshook, 1991,1989; Altfeld, 1988; Conybeare, 1994; Morrow, 1986). There are many motivations for alliance formation. However, most alliances have some security motivations. It is these security concerns that I consider. Rather than examine alliance formation, alliance reliability, and the effect of alliances as separate questions, I model these three aspects simultaneously. Nations use alliances to signal their intention to intervene should a war occur. Although not all alliances are reliable, upon seeing an alliance nations anticipate that intervention is likely. Alliance formation deters A and reassures B. This means that less attacks occur if an alliance forms. However, once an attack takes place, it is more likely that war occurs because B is less likely to surrender. Alliances are therefore related to the occurrence of war. When nations form alliances they do so knowing the reliability of the alliance, and the likely effect of alliance formation on the behavior of other states. In the next section, I describe a formal model of crisis behavior and alliance formation. Examining the crisis model reveals how the anticipated behavior in one nation affects the behavior of other nations. I describe how a third party's decision to intervene in a conflict, a protege's decision to resist an attack and an aggressor's decision to attack are related. Thus, the model relates the probability of intervention with the probability of attack and the probability of resistance. Specifically, the model shows that the more likely the third party, C, is to intervene the more likely the protege is to resist. This increase in intervention and retaliation reduces the probability that an aggressor will attack. These results have important consequences for evaluating empirical evidence. Empirical Implications The propositions above mean that as nation C becomes more likely to intervene nation A is less likely to attack and nation B is more likely to retaliate. These predictions, while not surprising, have profound empirical implications. In this section I explore these consequences. For brevity, I restrict discussion to the allied case, with the understanding that similar results hold in the non-allied case. Nation A does not attack all nations with the same probability. The propositions above shows that nation A is most likely to attack irresolute nations with unreliable allies. Resolute nations (high B) with reliable allies (high y) are not attractive targets for aggression. If A attacks these states then it is likely to find itself involved in a multilateral war. This is not to say that A never attacks Tesolute states with reliable allies; providing A anticipates the war going well (high z ), it might be prepared to fight multilateral wars. Nation A attacks in those situations where it expects to be successful. As Siverson and Gartner (1996; see also Siverson, 1995; Wang and Ray, 1994; Smith, 1996) show initiators win most of the conflicts they start. The results of the model suggest that this is not because initiators have an inherent advantage. Rather initiators pick the fights in which they anticipate a successful conclusion. A attacks when the probability of retaliation, B, and the probability of intervention, y, are low. What are the empirical consequences of this? In order to answer this question systematically, consider a set of international events. Without becoming engaged in how to operationalize variables, suppose some events end in acquiesce, other lead to war and the remainder are status quo events. Suppose we are interested in explaining B and C's behavior. Unfortunately, we only observe B's behavior if A attacks. However, the model show that A's decision depends upon the probability that B retaliates. Therefore, the majority of events where A tests B's resolve, by attacking, are events where B is likely to back down. When alliances are reliable and B will resist, attacks are unlikely. Unfortunately, these selection effects make statistical analysis difficult (Fearon, 1996). Suppose we want to examine alliance reliability, y. The consequence of selecting on the dependent variable is that the standard econometric assumption that the expected value of the stochastic error term is zero is violated (Achen, 1986; Dubin and Rivers, 1989; Greene, 1990; Heckman, 1976, 1979). Therefore, estimates of alliance reliability obtained using standard statistical procedure (such as probit) are biased. The Alliance/War Relationship The formation of an alliance has predictable effects on the behavior of the potential defender, the target and the aggressor. However, despite this, there is no general relationship between the formation of an alliance and the occurrence of war. The probability of war is a/3. Alliance formation reduces the probability of A attacking. This reduces the likelihood of war. However, alliance formation also increases the probability that B will retaliate if attacked. This increases the likelihood of war. In some situations alliance formation may increase the probability of war. Yet, in other situations alliance formation, may reduce the probability of armed conflict. In general, it is ambiguous whether a(BC)f3(BC) or a(0)/3(0) is larger. In the other equilibria either case is possible. In order to determine whether alliance formation increases or decreases the probability of war, it is necessary to examine the conditions to see which equilibria are likely to exist. If alliance formation has a larger predicted effect on nation A than nation B, then alliance formation is likely to result in a lower probability of war, and vice versa. This result suggests that empirical tests looking at the correlation between alliance formation and the occurrence of war are unlikely to find an aggregate effect. On the whole these empirical tests have been inconclusive (Singer and Bueno de Mesquita, 1973; Ostrom and Hoole, 1978; Wayman, 1990; Levy, 1984; King, 1989). Although the aggregate effect of alliances on the occurrence of war is ambiguous, the effect of alliances on aggressor and target nations is predictable. CONCLUSIONS Alliance formation reassures friends, making them more likely to retaliate. Alliance formation also deters enemies, making them less likely to attack. Alliance formation affects the behavior of aggressors and targets because it alters the expected behavior of third parties. Specifically, an alliance increases the probability of intervention should a war occur. This paper relates alliance formation to a model of war in an incomplete information setting. From this model, I develop a theory to explain the effects of alliances. The theory predicts how alliances affect the behavior of nations, and when nations form alliances. Deductive modeling is important in interpreting empirical evidence. This paper provides an interpretation of existing empirical evidence. It also provides new testable hypotheses. Modeling specifies the relationships to be tested. This allows the application of suitable econometric techniques, and suggests when traditional tests are inappropriate.
